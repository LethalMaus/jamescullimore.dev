Permission Slips and Flag Waving: Navigating the Android Development Skyline

By James Cullimore • Feb 14, 2024 • 6 min read

As we navigate the bustling streets of the Android development ecosystem, we pass through the dynamic metropolis of permissions and window flags. Imagine walking beside an experienced Android developer, their eyes glittering with code stories, obstacles, and solutions. Today, we’ll look at the complex relationship between permissions and window flags, two key components that influence the Android user experience.

Android applications’ access and functionality are governed by permissions, which function similarly to traffic lights. These rights, which range from accessing the device’s camera to reading external storage, are critical to protecting users’ security and privacy. In our journey around this digital metropolis, we will discover the 20 most common permissions, investigate their uses, and provide Kotlin code examples that demonstrate their use in real-world circumstances.

As our adventure progresses, we come across towers of window flags — markers that govern the look and behaviour of Android application windows. Window flags are the builders of our app’s visual environment, influencing everything from status and navigation bar display to screen orientation. In the next chapters, we will look at the ten most frequent window flags, using Kotlin code samples to demonstrate their importance in creating smooth and intuitive user interfaces.

So tighten your seatbelts as we go into the urban jungle of Android development, learning the tales behind the permissions and window flags that form the dynamic and engaging experiences we build for users. Join us on this journey where code becomes a language and each line adds to the symphony of a well-designed Android application.

Unveiling the Android Permissions Tapestry

INTERNET

What it does: Allows the app to create network sockets and use custom network protocols.

Where it’s needed: Almost every app requiring network communication.

Where it isn’t: Rare instances when an app operates solely offline.

CAMERA

What it does: Grants access to the device’s camera for capturing photos and videos.

Where it’s needed: Camera applications, augmented reality apps, QR code scanners.

Where it isn’t: Non-camera related applications.

READ_EXTERNAL_STORAGE

What it does: Permits reading from external storage.

Where it’s needed: Apps dealing with file browsing, media players.

Where it isn’t: Apps without the need for external storage access.

WRITE_EXTERNAL_STORAGE

What it does: Allows writing to external storage.

Where it’s needed: File downloaders, apps saving user-generated content.

Where it isn’t: Apps without any data storage requirements.

ACCESS_FINE_LOCATION / ACCESS_COARSE_LOCATION

What it does: Provides access to the device’s precise or approximate location.

Where it’s needed: Maps, location-based services.

Where it isn’t: Apps that don’t require location information.

BLUETOOTH / BLUETOOTH_ADMIN

What it does: Enables Bluetooth connectivity and administration.

Where it’s needed: Apps managing Bluetooth connections.

Where it isn’t: Apps unrelated to Bluetooth functionality.

READ_CONTACTS

What it does: Allows reading the user’s contacts.

Where it’s needed: Contact management, social apps.

Where it isn’t: Apps without contact-related features.

WRITE_CONTACTS

What it does: Grants permission to modify the user’s contacts.

Where it’s needed: Apps that update or modify contact information.

Where it isn’t: Apps without contact modification features.

RECORD_AUDIO

What it does: Permits recording audio from the device’s microphone.

Where it’s needed: Voice recording apps, video conferencing.

Where it isn’t: Apps not dealing with audio recording.

READ_PHONE_STATE

What it does: Allows read access to the phone state, including the device’s IMEI.

Where it’s needed: Telephony-related apps, device management.

Where it isn’t: Apps unrelated to phone state information.

SEND_SMS

What it does: Allows sending SMS messages.

Where it’s needed: Messaging apps, automation apps.

Where it isn’t: Apps not handling SMS functionality.

RECEIVE_SMS

What it does: Permits the app to receive SMS messages.

Where it’s needed: SMS-based authentication, messaging apps.

Where it isn’t: Apps not dealing with incoming SMS.

READ_CALENDAR / WRITE_CALENDAR

What it does: Grants read and write access to the user’s calendar.

Where it’s needed: Calendar apps, scheduling applications.

Where it isn’t: Apps without calendar-related features.

NFC

What it does: Allows communication with NFC tags.

Where it’s needed: Apps facilitating NFC-based transactions.

Where it isn’t: Apps without NFC functionality.

WAKE_LOCK

What it does: Keeps the device awake, preventing it from entering sleep mode.

Where it’s needed: Apps with background processes requiring continuous execution.

Where it isn’t: Apps without background tasks.

VIBRATE

What it does: Permits the app to control the device’s vibration.

Where it’s needed: Apps with haptic feedback, notifications.

Where it isn’t: Apps without vibration-related features.

FLASHLIGHT

What it does: Allows control over the device’s flashlight.

Illuminating the Android Interface: Unveiling the 10 Most Common Window Flags

In the sprawling metropolis of Android development, window flags stand as the architectural blueprints, shaping the appearance and behavior of our applications. As we navigate through the digital skyline, let’s explore the ten most common window flags, shedding light on their functionalities and deciphering when and where they are essential or extraneous.

FLAG_FULLSCREEN

What it does: Enables full-screen mode, hiding the status and navigation bars.

Where it’s needed: Immersive media experiences, games.

Where it isn’t: Apps requiring constant access to status and navigation bars.

FLAG_LAYOUT_NO_LIMITS

What it does: Allows the window to extend beyond the screen boundaries.

Where it’s needed: Creative UI designs, edge-to-edge layouts.

Where it isn’t: Standard app layouts without unconventional design requirements.

FLAG_KEEP_SCREEN_ON

What it does: Keeps the screen on, preventing it from entering sleep mode.

Where it’s needed: Apps with continuous user engagement, like reading or video playback.

Where it isn’t: Apps where prolonged screen activity is unnecessary.

FLAG_SECURE

What it does: Prevents the window from being captured or recorded.

Where it’s needed: Secure content display, e.g., banking or authentication screens.

Where it isn’t: Screens without sensitive information.

FLAG_NOT_FOCUSABLE

What it does: Disables focus for the window, making it non-interactive.

Where it’s needed: Overlays, such as floating widgets or notifications.

Where it isn’t: Main application screens requiring user interaction.

FLAG_HARDWARE_ACCELERATED

What it does: Enables hardware acceleration for the window.

Where it’s needed: Graphics-intensive applications, games.

Where it isn’t: Simple UIs without demanding graphical elements.

FLAG_SPLIT_TOUCH

What it does: Allows multiple views to process touch events simultaneously.

Where it’s needed: Multi-touch applications, gaming interfaces.

Where it isn’t: Apps with single-touch interactions.

FLAG_LAYOUT_IN_SCREEN

What it does: Includes the status bar in the layout, making it part of the window.

Where it’s needed: Apps with custom status bar designs.

Where it isn’t: Standard apps relying on the system status bar.

FLAG_LAYOUT_INSET_DECOR

What it does: Adjusts the window’s layout to account for system window decorations.

Where it’s needed: Custom UIs that need to consider system decorations.

Where it isn’t: Apps with standard layouts unaffected by system decorations.

FLAG_TRANSLUCENT_STATUS / FLAG_TRANSLUCENT_NAVIGATION

What it does: Renders the status or navigation bar with a translucent background.

Where it’s needed: Apps with immersive designs, blending with the system bars.

Where it isn’t: Apps requiring opaque status and navigation bars.
